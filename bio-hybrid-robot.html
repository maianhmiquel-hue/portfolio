This bio-hybrid robot houses living mycelium, whose electrical activity is measured and translated into text. When a visitor lays a hand on it, a language model writes a letter whose coherence depends on the state of the mycelium at that moment. As the organism declines over several days, these goodbye letters slowly fall apart until they can no longer be read. Neither clearly a machine nor a living being, this ambiguous robot blurs the categories through which we separate the living from the technical, and questions the affective relationships we are increasingly invited to build with technological objects.

Most of the machines around us are designed to disappear behind their function, to answer quickly and to resemble us a little more with each generation. This robot stems from the opposite intention. Its form borrows from the long history of robots without settling on any of them, and keeps its distance from the human figure as well as from any recognisable animal. Some see an insect in it, others an unknown creature or a simple apparatus. The robot keeps this doubt open, leaving each visitor to decide what they are facing.

The encounter begins with touch. Where technology usually asks to be commanded through buttons, screens or voice, the only possible gesture here is to lay a hand on soft white wool, closer to a caress than to an instruction. In return, the robot writes. Language models are generally made to serve and to please, to answer our questions and agree with us. Here nobody asks for anything. The letter arrives unrequested, addressed to whoever stands in front of the object, and its origin remains uncertain, somewhere between a living organism, a machine and the person who designed them.

Inside, the mycelium grows and slowly dies at its own pace, on a timescale no one controls. Every visitor meets it at a different moment of its life, and each letter carries a trace of that moment. Industrial objects are designed to wear out and be replaced, whereas this robot has a finitude of its own, and its words fade with it.

The letter can be read on the spot, left behind or taken home. By presenting a machine that one hesitates to touch, might wish to care for, or keeps a trace of, the robot brings to light the attachments that already bind us to technological objects, and opens onto other ways of living with machines, in which the relationship counts as much as the function.

The robot was designed and studied as part of the research presented in Strange Robots.
